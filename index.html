02 / Read the light 1 minute

What is a spectrum?

A spectrum shows how much light comes at different wavelengths. Wavelength is the distance from one wave crest to the next. Some light has wavelengths our eyes cannot see.

READ → Left to right: wavelength gets longer. Up: stronger output at that wavelength.

Ultraviolet (UV) is beyond violet, on the left. Infrared (IR) is beyond red, on the right. The rainbow between them is visible light.

The peak is the highest point of the curve. A peak shows the strongest part of this spectrum. Light also comes from wavelengths on both sides.

03 / A useful model 1 minute

A strange word: blackbody

A blackbody is an ideal model. It absorbs all light that hits it. The radiation it gives off depends on its temperature.

“Blackbody” does not mean it must look black. A hot one glows! In this model, a hotter patch of surface gives off more energy each second.

Useful does not mean perfect

Stars are not perfect blackbodies. Their real spectra have dips where gases absorb some light. A smooth curve still helps us study light given off by their hot surfaces.

A SMALL HISTORY OF A BIG IDEA

In the 1800s, scientists measured the light from hot objects more carefully. Wilhelm Wien found a pattern: hotter objects have their strongest output at shorter wavelengths. Today, you will test that pattern without using his equation.

08 / Solve the Sun mystery 3 minutes

A peak is not the whole spectrum.

The Sun’s curve peaks near the green part of the visible range on this wavelength graph. But it also gives off lots of blue, red, and other visible light.

Our eyes combine this broad mix as white. The Sun is not green, and it does not make only yellow light.

Earth’s air scatters some light. More blue light can be scattered out of our direct view, especially near sunrise or sunset. The Sun can then appear yellow, orange, or red.

COMPARE TWO DIFFERENT KINDS OF LIGHT

The broad visible mix is perceived as white.

09 / Stars have lives 4 minutes

The surface and the core
tell different parts of the story.

Different stars, different masses

Mass is how much matter a star has. More mass means stronger inward gravity. This affects core pressure, core temperature, and fusion.

During the main sequence, more massive stars generally have hotter surfaces and use fuel faster. They have shorter lives. Two stars can have different colors while both fuse hydrogen in their cores.

One star, changing over time

Fusion joins the centers of small atoms and releases energy. For most of a star’s life, core fusion turns hydrogen into helium.

When core hydrogen runs low, the star’s structure changes. Depending on its mass, it may later fuse helium or heavier elements. Not every star fuses the same elements.

As a star ages, its outer layers can expand or shrink. Its surface temperature and total light output can change. It can move to a different place on an H–R diagram.

Antares has a huge, relatively cool outer surface. Its deep interior is still extremely hot.

Fusion happens deep inside. The color we see mainly tells us about the surface.
